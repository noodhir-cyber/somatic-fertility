Chapter one · Days 1–12

Encouraging the eggs

In a natural cycle the ovaries release a single egg. During IVF, a daily hormone injection — follicle-stimulating hormone, or FSH — gently encourages the ovaries to mature several eggs at once, giving the best chance of creating healthy embryos.

The needle is finer than a sewing pin. Most people give the injections themselves at home, at roughly the same time each evening, for eight to twelve days.

  • 8–12days of injections
  • ~30 seach evening

Chapter two · Days 5–12

Watching them grow

Every few days an ultrasound scan and a blood test track the follicles — the small fluid-filled sacs on the ovary in which each egg matures.

When the largest follicles reach about 18 millimetres, a final “trigger” injection ripens the eggs. Collection is booked for exactly 36 hours later.

  • 18–20 mmfollicle size at trigger
  • 36 hfrom trigger to collection

Chapter three · Day 14

Gathering the eggs

Under light sedation, a fine needle guided by ultrasound passes into each follicle and gently draws out the fluid — and with it, the egg.

The whole procedure takes twenty to thirty minutes. Most people rest for an hour or two and go home the same day. The eggs travel a few metres down the corridor to the embryology lab.

  • 20–30 minprocedure
  • 8–15eggs collected on average

Chapter four · Day 14, in the lab

Where life begins

Each mature egg now meets sperm. In conventional IVF they are simply placed together in a dish. With ICSI — intracytoplasmic sperm injection — an embryologist selects a single healthy sperm and places it directly inside the egg using a glass needle thinner than a human hair.

Within hours, the egg and sperm each form a pronucleus. When two appear, fertilisation has happened.

  • 0.1 mmthe egg, our largest cell
  • 7 µmwidth of the injection pipette

Chapter five · Days 15–19

Growing, cell by cell

Day 1 · Zygote

The embryos rest in an incubator that mirrors the body — 37 °C, precise gases, complete darkness — while a camera watches every division.

One cell becomes two, then four, then eight. By day five the embryo is a blastocyst: a hollow ball of a hundred cells or more, with an inner cluster destined to become the baby and an outer layer that will form the placenta.

Chapter six · Day 19

Coming home

The healthiest embryo — usually a single blastocyst — is drawn into a soft, thin catheter and guided through the cervix into the uterus, with ultrasound showing the way.

There is no anaesthetic and no recovery. It feels much like a smear test and takes a few minutes. Any other good-quality embryos can be frozen for the future.

  • 1embryo transferred, usually
  • 5 minand you can walk out

Chapter seven · Days 20–33

The two-week wait

Over the next few days the blastocyst hatches from its shell and nestles into the lining of the uterus. Progesterone supports the lining as the connection forms.

Nine to fourteen days after transfer, a blood test measures hCG — the pregnancy hormone. This is the wait every family remembers.

  • Day 6–10after fertilisation: implantation
  • hCGthe hormone the test looks for

Chapter eight · Weeks 6–40

A heartbeat at six weeks

Week 8 · the size of a raspberry

Around six weeks after transfer, the first scan shows a flicker of heartbeat. From here, care passes to routine antenatal services — the pregnancy continues just like any other.
